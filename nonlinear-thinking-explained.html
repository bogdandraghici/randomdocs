A field guide to a Claude Code skill

Non‑Linear
Thinking

A guided way of exploring a hard decision instead of instantly answering it. It forces the conversation to open the space wide before it is allowed to narrow — and it puts a locked door between the two.

What  a set of instructions an AI follows For  decisions you keep circling Output  a written page you keep Runs  ~15–40 min
01 — WHY IT EXISTS

The problem with just asking

Most conversations about a decision go the same way — and that way quietly throws the interesting thinking in the bin.

Imagine you ask an assistant: “Should I rewrite this program or just clean it up?” It gives you a few options, you pick one, done. Fast and satisfying. But two harmful things just happened without anyone noticing.

The usual path

describe → options → pick
Your question → A few options → You choose

Feels efficient. It answers the question exactly as you asked it — which is precisely the trap.

What it costs you

two hidden harms
A.

It accepts your framing. The question you arrive with is a squashed-down conclusion — you already threw away every other option before you opened your mouth. “A or B?” hides the fact that C, D and E were quietly eliminated.

B.

It anchors you. Research on people working with AI shows we latch onto the model’s first suggestion and stop looking. The first idea becomes the anchor, and everything after is a tweak of it.

This skill is built to prevent both. It deliberately switches off the AI’s instinct to be helpful-and-quick, because that instinct is exactly what stops good exploration. You can get a fast recommendation from any chat — so if you invoked this, you wanted the harder, wider thing.

02 — THE ONE BIG IDEA

Open all the way up. Then — and only then — narrow.

Two mental modes, kept strictly apart. Generating ideas and judging ideas are different jobs, and doing them at once means you kill good ideas before they’re fully born.

Divergence — pour out ideas, good and terrible, all equal The Gate — a locked door; nothing is judged until you unlock it Convergence — now judgment switches on

Without a hard wall between generating and judging, the whole thing collapses back into ordinary brainstorming.

03 — THE PROCESS

Six phases, start to finish

The session moves through fixed stages. Warm colours are the “open up” half; cool colours are the “narrow down” half; the red band is the wall between them.

0

Intake & sizing setup

Get the problem in your own words. Figure out what kind of problem it is, and agree on how long to spend — a full ~40-minute session, a compressed ~15-minute one, or just “framing only.” It also checks: is this really a thinking problem, or do you just need a fact you can go look up? If a single missing number would settle it, it says so and offers to stop.

1

Frame interrogation open

Attack the question before answering it. Your question is a compressed conclusion — so it gets taken apart. Sometimes the real question turns out to be completely different, and the session can honourably end right here, having handed you a better question instead of an answer.

2

Divergence open

Generate options using at least two techniques from different families (see below). One technique per message, run properly. No evaluation of any kind — no “that one’s clever,” no ranking, not even listing them best-first. Weak and absurd ideas stay on the board with equal standing.

3

The Gate locked door

A hard stop. The full list is laid out, flat and unranked, and you are asked point-blank whether you’re done generating. Nothing gets judged until you personally say “go.” The AI is forbidden from crossing this line on its own judgment — that is the single most important rule in the whole skill.

4

Convergence narrow

Now judgment turns on. Group the options by what they actually do, test them against the reframed problem from Phase 1 (not the one you walked in with), cut them using criteria you name, then stress-test the front-runner by imagining it has already failed.

5

The artifact keep

Everything is written to a dated Markdown file — the question you arrived with, the reframed one, every option (including the discarded ones and why), and the facts that would settle it. Designed so you — or a future session — can pick it up cold, months later.

The load-bearing piece

Why the Gate has to be locked

The moment you allow even a whisper of judgment during idea-generation — “ooh, that one’s good” — people stop generating and start defending the favourite. The Gate is a literal checkpoint: the AI stops, lays out the whole board unranked, and asks you a fixed question.

“Are you done diverging, or do you want to push further? Nothing gets judged until you say go.”

Crucially, only a clear “yes” counts. The skill is strict about what does and doesn’t open the door:

✓ Crosses the gate

An affirmative close — “done,” “go,” “converge,” “yes, judge them.” Only this unlocks judgment.

✗ Does not cross

Passing the decision back: “you tell me,” “whatever you think,” “I guess so.” Also: silence, a topic change, or excitement about one option. The door stays shut.

If you keep deferring, it won’t decide for you. It names the deadlock and gives you a binary: say go and it starts judging, or say nothing more and it writes up what you have and stops. It never crosses on its own hunch.

“Just tell me which one you’d pick!”

If you ask for the AI’s opinion mid-session, it deflects — once — and tells you exactly why:

“I have a view. Holding it is the point — if I name it now it becomes the anchor and you stop generating. Ask me again once we’ve cut the board down.”

You can always overrule it and end the session for a straight answer — but that has to be an explicit choice, not something the AI talks itself into. And even then it writes up the session first: switching drops the process, never the record.

04 — THE TOOLKIT

Five families of technique

These are the actual moves used to prise open the option space. The rule is to draw from different families — because two techniques of the same type produce the same kind of answer. Which one gets used depends on the shape of your problem, not its subject.

01

Frame-shifting

attack the question itself
  • Dig up hidden assumptions
  • “What would have to be true for A to be obviously right?”
  • Zoom in / zoom out (altitude)
  • Regret minimisation
The move Your one-line question hides a dozen buried assumptions. Excavate them, and “A or B?” usually stops being a two-item menu.
02

Random-stimulus

a way in from outside the rut
  • Random word entry
  • Provocation — say something deliberately false, then walk it back to something real
The move Pull a genuinely random word (with a shuffle command — no cheating by picking one that “fits”). Its odd attributes become a doorway your normal thinking would never have reached.
03

Combinatorial

recombine, don’t just recall
  • Morphological grids (parameters × settings)
  • SCAMPER prompts
  • Analogical transfer
The move Break your options into independent “knobs.” Four knobs with five settings each is 625 combinations — and you arrived with three of them.
04

Systems

for problems that keep coming back
  • Causal loops (with signs and delays)
  • Second-order effects — “and then what?”
  • Constraint archaeology
  • Sorting simple vs. complex problems
The move A problem you’ve “fixed” three times is usually a loop pushing back. Map the arrows and the delays, and the real lever appears.
05

Adversarial

attack it before reality does
  • Premortem — “it’s failed; write the story of how”
  • Inversion — “how would I guarantee this fails?”
  • Red team & steelman
The move Imagining a failure that already happened produces far more specific, honest causes than asking “what could go wrong?”
✦

How it chooses

by structure, not subject

A career decision and a software-architecture decision with the same shape get the same treatment. What matters is the pattern underneath — and each technique lives in its own reference file, loaded only when it’s actually chosen.

05 — READING THE SIGNATURE

What you say → what’s really wrong

The very first job is diagnosis. The words you use point to a “signature,” and the signature — not the topic — decides which techniques come out.

What you saySignatureWhat’s actually wrong
“Should I do A or B?”decisionA false either/or — the space was collapsed before you started
“Every idea is a variant of the same thing”stuckFixation — your mind keeps running downhill into the same rut
“I keep going back and forth”decisionA clash of values, not a shortage of information
“This keeps coming back”recurringYou’re treating a symptom; a system is restoring the old state
“I need lots of options”generativeNothing wrong — you just need volume
“I think X is right but I’m not sure”decisionYou’re seeking confirmation, not examination
“Too many factors, it’s overwhelming”recurringSeveral different problems glued together as one

Note: it deliberately will not fire on factual questions, requests for a straight recommendation, or debugging (“why is this broken”). Those are different jobs.

06 — HOW IT ASKS

Six rules for asking honestly

The bottleneck isn’t clever techniques — it’s the depth of your answers. People arrive with a rehearsed, comfortable version of their problem. These rules deny you the easy path back to it.

1

One question at a time

Multiple questions in a message means the easy one gets answered and the hard one — always the important one — gets dropped forever.

2

The second-answer rule

Your first answer is rehearsed — the story you already tell yourself. So it asks again: “That’s the version you’ve worked out. What’s underneath it?”

3

Ask, then stop

No cushioning, no examples. Offering candidate answers turns an open question into multiple-choice — and you’ll pick the AI’s option instead of finding your own.

4

Use your exact words

If you say “gross,” it says “gross” back — never “technical debt.” Every tidy translation smuggles in the AI’s framing and replaces your problem with its own.

5

Specifics, not categories

“What went wrong last time?” — not “what tends to go wrong?” A real memory contains the details that spark new options; a category is safe and useless.

6

Notice avoidance

What you steer away from marks the live wire. It doesn’t call it out in the moment — it quietly returns to it later, once, from a different angle.

07 — SEEING IT WORK

One technique, start to finish

The clearest way to understand this is to watch a single move play out. Here’s the “random word” technique — used when someone is completely stuck in one pattern.

The stuck problem

“Our meal-kit subscription loses customers after month three.”

“And every single idea anyone has had is a discount. Cheaper first box, cheaper fourth box, win-back discount, loyalty discount. I can’t get us off it.”  — A textbook fixation.

1

Draw a genuinely random word

Not chosen — drawn, with a shuffle command, so the AI can’t sneak in one that already fits. A word that “fits” came from the rut you’re trying to escape.

$ shuf → tourniquet

2

Mine the word — before touching the problem

Spend a minute on the word alone. What’s true about it that people never think about?

“Hold the retention thing for a second. The word is tourniquet. What is it, what does it do?”
“Stops bleeding. You put it above the wound, not on it. You write the time on the patient — leave it too long and they lose the limb. It’s the last thing you try, not the first. And it hurts.”
3

Force one attribute against the problem — repeat

Take a single attribute and press it onto the problem. Then the next. The first connection is a warm-up; the good ones come later.

“‘You write the time on them.’ Put that next to your discounts.”
“…None of ours have an end. We’ve got customers 18 months in on a code we ran for one week in 2023.”
4

Everything lands on the board — unjudged

Six connections, none of them a discount. They go on the list in the order they came, flat, no “this one’s best.” The word itself never appears on the board — it was just scaffolding.

The board · generation order · nothing ranked
  1. Every retention offer carries a written expiry and a review date
  2. Move the fix to the incident (the bad box in month one), not the cancel screen
  3. Deliberately let the discount-hunting segment go
  4. Build the missing ladder of non-price responses; discount is the last rung
  5. Let the driver or support agent fix a bad delivery on the spot
  6. Charge the cost back to the team whose mistake caused it

The original thinking had exactly one axis: how much money we give back, and when. None of these six sit on it. That is the point of the whole exercise.

→

…and only later, past the Gate, does any of it get judged

The board grows with a second technique from a different family, hits the Gate, and — once you say “go” — gets clustered, tested, and cut down into a shaped set of real options, written up in a file you keep.

08 — WHAT YOU WALK AWAY WITH

Not a verdict — a shaped space

The output isn’t “do X.” It’s a written page: a smaller set of live options, the reasons the others are gone, and the exact facts that would decide between what remains. Here’s the skeleton of that file.

thinking/2026-07-27-developer-champion-deals.md
# The question I arrived with
“Should we build a public API?”
# The question after reframing
“How do we stop losing deals where the champion is a developer who never gets to try the product?”
# Assumptions surfaced
| assumption | load-bearing? | survived? |
# Techniques run
| technique | family | stimulus | what it produced |
# The full option space
  Live candidates — one line each, on what it does (not why it's good)   Discarded — and why | option | cut by which of YOUR criteria | note |
# Premortem: leading candidate
| failure cause (past tense) | earliest visible signal |
# What would change my mind
- a checkable fact — and where it would show up first
# Open threads
- questions raised and not resolved, with enough context to pick up cold

The two sections that feel redundant on the day — Discarded & why and Open threads — are the only ones that matter in six weeks. Without them, a future session just regenerates the same options and cuts them again against criteria nobody remembers.

The session’s job isn’t to hand you the answer.
It’s to make sure you saw the whole board before you chose.
